What should a peptide COA include before buying from China?

China Partner Hub · Updated 2026-07-13

Supplier verification

Q: What should a peptide COA include before buying from China?

A peptide COA should let you connect one physical batch to a clear product identity, a stated specification, and actual test results. It should not be treated as a generic quality certificate. A COA is usually issued by the supplier or manufacturer, so its value depends on whether the document matches the material, the batch, and the tests that matter for your intended use.

Start with identity. The document should show the exact product name, relevant identifier for the category, grade or intended use, batch or lot number, manufacture date, retest or expiry date, and storage conditions. The supplier name and manufacturing site should also be clear. If you cannot tell whether the COA refers to the material in the quote, it cannot support a buying decision.

Next, look at the specification and result side by side. Each important test should show a method or reference, an acceptance range, and the batch result. "Pass" is not enough when the number itself matters. For a defined peptide ingredient, that may include identity confirmation, assay or purity, moisture or water content, residual solvents, and relevant impurity limits. The exact panel changes with the peptide and its use, so do not copy a checklist from an unrelated supplier.

For collagen peptides and other food or supplement ingredients, the useful evidence is different. Buyers may need source information, molecular-weight distribution, amino-acid profile, protein content, microbiological limits, heavy metals, allergens, and destination-market food documentation. A cosmetic raw material may need INCI naming, appearance, solubility or formulation guidance, microbiological controls where relevant, and stability information. These documents should not be substituted for one another simply because both products use the word "peptide."

Check the batch logic carefully. The batch number on the COA should match the sample, retained sample, packing label, invoice, and shipment records where possible. Ask whether the sample tested was taken from the same lot that will ship. A supplier can show a genuine report from an old good batch while sending a different one later. That is why a COA is stronger when the order has traceable lot control.

An independent lab report can add confidence, but it needs the same scrutiny. Check the lab identity, report number, sample description, sample receipt date, test method, and whether the report is complete rather than a cropped screenshot. If the result is commercially important, verify that the lab and report are real and that the sampled material is connected to your order.

The mistake is asking for a COA as a formality. Use it as a document-control tool: it should tell you what the material is, which batch you are buying, what was tested, and what evidence you have if a later result does not match. If it cannot do that, ask for better documentation before you release payment.
