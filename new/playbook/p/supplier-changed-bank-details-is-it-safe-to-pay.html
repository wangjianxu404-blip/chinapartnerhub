My supplier wants payment sent to a Hong Kong account or third-party export company - is that normal?

China Partner Hub · Updated 2026-06-18

Payment & terms Supplier verification

The situation

"The supplier I have been talking to says payment should go to a Hong Kong account, or to a separate export company, instead of the mainland company name I thought I was dealing with. Sometimes they say this is normal for tax, export, or foreign-currency reasons. Is that actually normal, or am I about to lose control of the transaction?"

Short answer

It can be normal, but it is never something to accept casually. The issue is not whether the receiving account is in Hong Kong or under another export entity. The real issue is whether the payment structure is transparent, documented, and consistent with the transaction you thought you were making. If the beneficiary, seller, and manufacturer no longer line up clearly, your risk rises sharply.

How to think about it

  1. Separate trade structure from fraud signal. Some suppliers do use Hong Kong entities or third-party exporters for legitimate operational reasons. That alone does not prove a scam. The danger starts when the buyer does not know exactly why, who each entity is, and who is contractually responsible if something goes wrong.
  2. Watch for three mismatches. Beneficiary mismatch means the account name does not match the seller. Entity mismatch means the company on the invoice, contract, and bank details do not line up. Manufacturer mismatch means the factory behind the goods is not the company you thought it was. One mismatch may be explainable. Several together usually mean slow down.
  3. Ask for the structure in writing before paying. If a third-party exporter is involved, you should know who sells to you, who manufactures, who exports, who receives payment, and who carries liability for quality, timing, and document errors.
  4. Treat last-minute change as different from early transparency. If the structure is explained clearly from the beginning, documented, and consistent, it may be manageable. If the account appears late, under pressure, with vague reasons, treat it as a red flag until verified.
  5. Do not confuse convenience with protection. A supplier saying "this is normal" does not reduce your risk. Normal for them and safe for you are not the same thing.

Specifics

  • Ask for: full legal names of all entities involved, explanation of each entity's role, matching invoice and contract details, export-document consistency, and confirmation of who is liable if goods, documents, or quality fail.
  • Higher-risk signs: personal account, unexplained Hong Kong beneficiary, new beneficiary introduced after sampling, pressure to pay quickly, inconsistent explanations from sales contacts, and refusal to document the structure.
  • A good rule: if the money path is less clear than the product path, stop and clarify before sending funds.
