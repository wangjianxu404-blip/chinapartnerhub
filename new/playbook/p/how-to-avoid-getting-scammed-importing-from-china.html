What's the best way to avoid getting scammed when importing products from China?

China Partner Hub · Updated 2026-06-29

Supplier verification Payment & terms

The situation

"I keep hearing stories about fake suppliers, disappearing deposits, wrong goods, and factories that turn out not to be what they claimed. What is the best practical way to avoid getting scammed when importing from China?"

Short answer

The best way to avoid getting scammed when importing from China is not one trick. It is a layered control system: verify the supplier, lock the specification, control payment exposure, create independent visibility during production, and scale only after the supplier proves repeatability.

Most losses do not come from dramatic movie-style fraud. They come from smaller avoidable gaps: wrong payee, unclear factory responsibility, vague specs, weak QC gates, and trust moving faster than verification.

How to think about it

  1. Define scam risk broadly. Sometimes the supplier is fake. More often, the company exists but the chain behind the order is unclear, the goods do not match the promise, or the buyer loses control after deposit.
  2. Verify the supplier before discussing scale. Confirm the legal company identity, check whether the bank account matches, understand whether you are dealing with a factory, trader, or mixed model, and check whether the product sits inside their real specialty.
  3. Lock the product specification before comparing quotes. Materials, dimensions, finish, packaging, labeling, and compliance requirements need to be clear. A vague spec creates a false sense of price certainty.
  4. Control payment exposure instead of treating deposit as trust. Avoid rushed wires, unexplained account changes, and weak milestones. The more money moves ahead of clarity, the more the buyer loses leverage.
  5. Create independent visibility before shipment. Local follow-up, in-process checks, and pre-shipment inspection catch the gap between what was promised and what is actually being made.
  6. Scale in steps, not in hope. A small controlled order tells you more about quality consistency, communication, and lead-time discipline than another round of reassuring sales messages.

A practical anti-scam stack

  • Verify company identity before paying anything meaningful.
  • Confirm the bank account and payment chain.
  • Lock the product specification before comparing quotes.
  • Approve samples carefully, but do not confuse sample success with batch safety.
  • Tie production follow-up and inspection to payment milestones.
  • Keep final leverage until the batch actually passes your release standard.

Specifics

  • Common failure points: wrong payee, hidden middleman, weak specification, no sample discipline, no independent check before shipment.
  • Strong warning signs: pressure to pay fast, changing bank details, vague answers on in-house versus outsourced work, a very attractive quote with weak technical answers, or a broad catalog with no clear specialization.
  • A good sample is useful, but it is not enough. It proves one acceptable piece, not stable execution.

The best question to ask yourself before each payment

What new verified information do I have now that I did not have before the last payment?

If the answer is "not much, but the supplier keeps reassuring me," you are probably increasing risk, not reducing it.
