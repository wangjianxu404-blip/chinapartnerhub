Before Paying a Chinese Supplier: 6 Checks That Expose Real Risk

The useful checks are not badges or promises. They are the points where the legal company, seller, payee, factory, certificate, sample, and bulk-order plan must describe the same transaction.

Most supplier problems do not begin with an obviously fake company. They begin when different parts of the deal do not line up: the business license is real, but the PI names another seller; the sample is good, but nobody confirms who will make the bulk order; the certificate is genuine, but it belongs to another company or product.

Before advising a buyer to move forward, these are the six transaction chains I compare.

1

Does the legal seller match the payment chain?

Start with the full Chinese company name on the business license. Compare it with the seller on the quotation, PI, or contract and with the beneficiary on the bank account. A Hong Kong company or export agent can be legitimate, but its role must be explained in writing. A real license for Company A does not make a payment to unexplained Company B safe.

2

Who will actually make this order?

Do not stop at “Are you a factory?” Ask which legal entity operates the site, which production steps are in-house, what will be subcontracted, and where the product will be made. Then connect the operating address, workshop evidence, relevant machinery, and current in-process goods to your product. A video of a factory is not useful if it cannot be tied to the supplier and order under review.

3

Can the quote be explained by the same specification?

A low quote is not automatically false, and a round number does not reveal whether the seller is a factory. The useful question is where the price difference comes from. Compare material grade, dimensions, finish, component brand, packaging, tooling, defect allowance, inspection, and freight assumptions. If two suppliers are not pricing the same specification and responsibility, the prices are not comparable.

4

Will the bulk order follow the approved sample?

A sample proves that one acceptable unit existed. It does not prove that the same workshop, material, component, or process will be used for the bulk order. Record the approved sample reference, specification, BOM or material details, tolerances, finish, packaging, and change-approval rule. Also confirm who made the sample and who will make the production batch.

5

Do the certificates belong to this company and product?

A certificate can be genuine and still be irrelevant. Check the certificate holder, manufacturer, product name or model, applicable standard, test scope, issuing laboratory, validity date, and production site where relevant. A PDF forwarded by the salesperson is not proof that the supplier or the product you are buying is covered.

6

What leverage remains after each payment?

Map the deposit, production milestones, inspection point, final-payment condition, and evidence required for release. Decide what happens if the supplier changes bank details, misses a specification, refuses inspection, or asks for the balance before the agreed evidence exists. Verification matters most when it changes what you pay, what you require, or whether you proceed.

The risk usually sits between the documents

Each item can look acceptable in isolation. The license can be real. The factory can exist. The certificate can be genuine. The sample can be good. The danger appears when they belong to different entities, products, sites, or production plans.

Do not ask only whether the supplier is “legitimate.” Ask whether the full transaction is clear enough to support the payment you are about to make. If one link cannot be explained, pause there and request evidence before the deposit reduces your leverage.
